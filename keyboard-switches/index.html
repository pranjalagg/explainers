    How a keyboard works

    1. Every key has two jobs.It has to push back on your finger, and it has to tell the controller it has been pressed. The seven switches on the bench do the first job with a spring, a ramp, a jacket, a dome or a buckling coil. They do the second with metal, light, a magnet or an electric field. The two are separate, and you can see that by watching the force chart and the signal chart move at different moments.
    2. The force curve is what you feel.It plots resistance against how far down the key is. A straight line is linear. A bump is tactile. A bump with a sudden drop is clicky. The line on the way down and the line on the way up never match, because of friction and because snaps happen at different points in each direction.
    3. The signal is what the computer sees.Contacts give a step that bounces. Light and capacitance give smooth curves that firmware compares with a threshold. A magnet gives a curve of field strength that is turned back into a depth. Where the signal crosses its threshold is the actuation point, and it does not have to be where you feel anything.
    4. The matrix finds out which key.A keyboard does not run a wire from every key. Keys sit at the crossings of a grid of rows and columns, and the controller drives one column at a time while reading the rows. With three keys down at the corners of a rectangle, current can sneak back through the third and a fourth key looks pressed. A diode in series with each switch, like the small black part on each board here, stops that. A membrane cannot carry diodes, so it blocks a third key instead. Capacitive and Hall switches pass no current and do not have the problem.

    Where the numbers come from

    Actuation points, forces and travel for the Cherry-style switches come from Cherry's own pages and the Deskthority wiki. Buckling spring figures come from the Wikipedia articles on the Model M and buckling spring, Admiral Shark's Keyboards and Unicomp. Topre figures come from Geekhack measurements and a Topre-focused guide, which disagree by a few tenths of a millimetre, so the figure uses the more conservative value.

    The optical and Hall effect switches, and the exact shape of every force curve, are illustrative: drawn through the published points, not measured from a product. RESEARCH.md in this folder lists every claim with its source and marks each number as published or illustrative, and node model.mjs --check prints how the curves compare with the ratings.
